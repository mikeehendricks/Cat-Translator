How it works (and how well it actually works)

The meow codec

A meow is described by four properties, so it is written as a token:

  • onset — m, prr (trilled), h (breathy) or none → "me", "prrme", "hme", "e"
  • vowel — a e i o u y, which sets the formants
  • tone — flat, rise ↑, fall ↓, arch ˆ, dip ˌ (a real pitch contour, in semitones)
  • length — short ·, normal, long :

Four specials exist too: purr, hiss, chirp (sharp rising call) and chatter (the machine-gun trill at birds). "thank you" is my↑ me↑; "no" is hu↓:.

Choosing the codebook

Both ends of this translator are mine, so which sound means which word was a design choice. Rather than hand-picking sounds that later turn out to be indistinguishable, the generator renders all 364 possible meows, measures a 33-number fingerprint of each, and picks the ~115 that sit farthest apart in that acoustic space. Each meaning keeps the tone and length of its original hand-written meow (falling = no/bad, rising = question/urgency) and only vowel and onset are reassigned for distinctness. Numbers are counted out as repeats of one counting meow, so a missed meow shifts the number instead of corrupting the sentence.

How the listening works

  • Audio is resampled to a canonical 22.05 kHz (44.1/48 kHz recordings come in from phones and laptops).
  • Energy gaps split the recording into individual meows.
  • Each meow becomes 33 numbers: pitch contour, range, duration, harmonicity, spectral flatness, a purr detector (18–45 Hz rumble), a trill detector (15–40 Hz flutter), plus 16 log-spaced bands measured after pitch-normalising the spectrum (so a deep voice lines up with the reference).
  • Pitch is tracked by a normalised autocorrelation on a 300 Hz–2.5 kHz band, so a 26 Hz purr cannot drag the estimate onto its own harmonics.
  • Every meow is summarised by 1,469 fingerprints rendered by the synthesiser itself, then treated as a small Gaussian (its own mean and spread per dimension) — so a meow that naturally wanders in one dimension stops shouting for that meow only. Weights come from a coordinate-ascent search on a held-out set.

Measured accuracy

Evaluated with fresh meows (unseen voice, speed, level, hiss, muffled microphone, 22/44/48 kHz capture), one to four meows per utterance:

91.7%per-meow top-1 (n=733)
97.8%per-meow top-3
90.9%word accuracy
80.7%whole utterance exact

That is why the app shows four candidates instead of pretending to be certain — and why you can tap the reading you meant, which re-decodes the whole sentence on the spot. "Read back the last meow", which skips the microphone, is nearly exact.

Adding one condition at a time to the same meows: identical to a reference call 99 %, a new voice or speed 100 %, a quiet recording 100 %, light hiss 100 %, a muffled microphone 99 %, a 48 kHz capture 98 % — and 16 kHz capture only 66 %, because a 16 kHz recording genuinely has no top octave. Phones and laptops capture at 44.1–48 kHz.

What this is not

Real cats don't have words. This is a constructed language you can hear: a codec where the speaker and the listener were designed together, so the round trip is genuinely decodable. Your cat is probably reacting to the pitch, the rhythm and your attention — which, to be fair, is most of what it ever wanted anyway.
